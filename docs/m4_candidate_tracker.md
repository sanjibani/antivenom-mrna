# M4: the candidate tracker

**In-silico designs. No therapeutic efficacy demonstrated. Not medical advice.**

This report maps the published antibody candidates that have a claimed neutralising activity against a toxin, or against the whole venom, of one of India's Big Four snakes. It is generated by `scripts/candidate_tracker.py` from `src/mrna/candidates.py`, so the table and the analysis cannot disagree with each other.

Two things are deliberately kept apart here. The first is whether a candidate works. The second is whether its sequence can be transcribed. A candidate can be excellent on the first count and unusable on the second, and for a project whose contribution is the mRNA layer, the second count is the one that decides.

## What the survey found

- Only 8 of 22 candidates have a variable-region sequence that can be located in a public database. Sequence availability, not antibody discovery, is the bottleneck for an mRNA programme.
- Common krait venom is about 64.5 per cent phospholipase A2 by mass, and no published antibody candidate in this survey targets krait phospholipase A2. 0 candidates cover krait phospholipase A2.
- No published antibody candidate targets the snake venom metalloproteinases of Indian Russell's viper, which are the main cause of the haemorrhage in that bite. 0 candidates cover that toxin family.
- 7 of the uncovered cells belong to a Big Four species, and every one of them is a cell with no candidate at all rather than a cell with weak candidates.
- The one construct this repository has built, for E113, is one of the candidates whose sequence is publicly available.

### An empty cell is not always an opportunity

Two things have to be read alongside the matrix. The first is that a candidate whose target toxin the source does not resolve is recorded with the toxin family `unspecified`, so it appears as no coverage anywhere. That is why the camelid antivenom raised against Indian saw-scaled viper venom does not fill the Echis carinatus sochureki row.

The second is that the Kunitz-type serine protease inhibitor cells are empty for both viper species, and that is probably correct rather than a hole. Those inhibitors are the anticoagulant fraction of viper venom: they bind the clotting factors directly. An antibody against them would protect the clotting cascade, which is the opposite of what a patient with a viper bite needs. A tracker that reported every empty cell as an opportunity would be pointing this project at a design that would make things worse, so the matrix has to be read with the toxin families in mind.

The rest are not missing because they are bad. They are missing because the sequence is in a paper's supplementary table, in a patent, or nowhere that a machine can read. That is a solvable problem, and it is a much smaller problem than discovering an antibody.

## Reading the toxin families

- **3FTx-L**: Long-chain three-finger toxins are the neurotoxins of the elapids. They block the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor and cause flaccid paralysis.
- **3FTx-S**: Short-chain three-finger toxins are the cytotoxins of the elapids. They damage cell membranes and cause the local tissue necrosis of a cobra bite.
- **PLA2**: Phospholipase A2 is the dominant toxin of Indian cobra and saw-scaled viper venom, and the dominant fraction of common krait venom by mass. It is myotoxic, hemolytic and anticoagulant at the same time.
- **SVMP**: Snake venom metalloproteinases are the haemorrhagic toxins of the viper bites. They degrade the basement membrane of small blood vessels, which is what produces the bleeding.
- **KSPI**: Kunitz-type serine protease inhibitors are the anticoagulant fraction of viper venom, because they inhibit the clotting factors directly. Neutralising them would make the coagulopathy worse rather than better, so an empty cell here may be a correct decision by the field rather than a hole in it.
- **LAAO**: L-amino acid oxidase contributes to the coagulopathy of viper bites by damaging the vessel wall rather than by acting on the clotting cascade itself.
- **mixed**: A candidate or a cocktail that covers more than one toxin family.
- **unspecified**: The source does not resolve the target toxin, so the row cannot be placed in the gap matrix.

## What the table does not claim

- **It is a survey, not an exhaustive census.** A row saying `none` for a sequence means the sequence was not located during the survey on 2026-11-01, not that no sequence exists.
- **Patent status is a fact about filings, not a clearance.** A row marked `application-pending` records that an application is public. It is not an opinion about freedom to operate, and it is not a freedom-to-operate opinion.
- **Evidence levels are the highest rung reached, not the quality of the result.** A candidate at L4 has protection data in an animal model. It does not follow that the protection was complete, or that the model resembles a human bite.
- **No candidate here has been evaluated by this project.** This is a map of what has been published. The one construct this repository has built, for E113, is built from a published sequence and carries the published data with it.

## Vocabularies

Evidence levels:

- `L0`: in silico only; no experimental data
- `L1`: expressed and purified
- `L2`: binding measured, in vitro
- `L3`: neutralisation measured, ex vivo or in cells
- `L4`: protection measured in an animal model
- `L5`: manufacturing, formulation or chemistry demonstrated

mRNA suitability:

- `ready`: a defined sequence is public and the format is a single open reading frame
- `adaptable`: a defined sequence is public, but the format needs more than one ORF or an engineering step
- `blocked`: no defined sequence exists to transcribe, because the candidate is polyclonal
- `unknown`: the sequence was not located, so nothing can be said about buildability

The suitability verdict is not a hand annotation. The engine recomputes it from the format and the sequence source and refuses to load a row where the two disagree, so the column cannot drift away from the facts it claims to summarise.
